Two first-round picks. That’s the price the Green Bay Packers paid to bring Micah Parsons to Lambeau Field. And Packers Nation, I think that one number explains almost everything about what happened at this year’s trade deadline.

Because while half the league was burning up the phone lines, Brian Gutekunst sat still. No trades before the deadline. No Jonathan Taylor. No offensive lineman. No splash. Nothing.

And a lot of fans are frustrated. I get it. We spent weeks on this channel talking about Landon Dickerson, Tony Pollard, Tyjae Spears, Josh Sweat, Vita Vea, Jonathan Taylor. We watched the Bears trade two first-rounders for Jeffery Simmons. We watched Seattle grab Quenton Nelson. We watched San Francisco add DeForest Buckner and Keenan Allen. We watched Kansas City pick up Josh Downs. And Green Bay? Silence.

But here’s the thing. The more I look at it, the more I’m convinced this decision wasn’t a mistake. It was a plan. And it might be one of the smartest things this front office has done all year.

Welcome back to Third Floor. Today we’re breaking down the quiet deadline. Why Gutekunst keeps doing this. Why it works more often than people admit. And why the best addition the Packers could possibly make in the second half of this season is already sitting on their own roster. Let’s get into it.

Let’s start with the pattern. Because this isn’t new. For a long time now, the Green Bay Packers haven’t been an active team at the trade deadline. And that’s not an accident. And it’s not laziness either.

This organization values two things above almost everything else. Draft capital. And the cheap rookie contracts that come with it. Those two things drive nearly every decision this front office makes.

Different teams operate in different ways. Some teams see a window and kick the door down. They trade picks, they take on salary, they go all in for one season. The Packers see a window and ask a different question. What is that door going to cost us three years from now?

And this franchise has a long history of success doing it this way. They’ve found ways to win, year after year, without making a habit of deadline blockbusters.

Let me put the philosophy in simple terms. Because I think a lot of the frustration on deadline day comes from fans measuring every team with the same ruler. And that ruler says a good front office is an active front office. The more trades, the better.

But activity isn’t the same as quality. A team can make five trades and still end up worse off, if it spent its future to patch up the present.

The Packers measure themselves with a different ruler. Their ruler asks, how many chances will we have to hit on cheap, young talent over the next several years? Every draft pick is a chance. Every chance is a possible starter on a bargain contract. And you don’t throw those chances away lightly.

Now, I’ll be the first to say it. If this team were struggling, this would be a very different conversation. If the Packers were sitting there with one glaring weakness that could cost them the whole season, I’d be the first one asking why nothing happened.

But that’s not where they are. They’re six and three. They’ve won five of their last six. The offensive line has gotten better as the year has gone on. The offense just scored forty-five points against the defending AFC champions in their own building. The defense is playing at a top-five level. And Micah Parsons hasn’t even come back yet.

When you add all that up, I just don’t see a desperate need that screamed for a trade partner.

Would it have been cool to see a move? Of course. Could they have improved the roster? No doubt. But every improvement at the deadline comes with a bill. And that bill gets paid in future draft picks.

And here’s the part I keep coming back to. You can find players in the fourth round who walk in and make a major impact. Just look at this roster.

Evan Williams. A fourth-round pick. And right now, Pro Football Focus has him graded as the number one safety in the entire NFL. He leads the team in tackles. He picked off Drake Maye against New England. He’s twenty-five years old and on a rookie contract through the end of 2027.

Dani Dennis-Sutton. Another fourth-round pick, taken at one hundred twenty overall in this year’s draft after posting a ridiculous relative athletic score of nine point nine six out of ten. And he’s played really well as a rookie, making plays in the backfield and getting after the quarterback.

Those are exactly the kinds of players the Packers want more chances to find. The more fourth-round picks they have, the more fifth-rounders, the more third-rounders, the more lottery tickets they’re holding. Lottery tickets on players who end up being starters on cheap deals.

And there’s a compounding effect here that people really underrate. A fourth-round pick who turns into a starter doesn’t just fill one spot for one season. He fills it for years, at a rookie price. And that savings is what lets the team keep its expensive stars. Multiply that by several picks, and you get a roster with real depth and real flexibility.

But trade those picks away, and you remove the engine that makes the whole machine run.

Think about what that means in practice. A rookie contract is the cheapest labor in football. When a fourth-round pick hits, you get a starting-caliber player at a price that frees up money to spend somewhere else. When you trade that pick for a half-season rental, you lose the lottery ticket. And you lose it whether the rental works out or not.

That’s the whole logic. And it’s why a team that looks passive from the outside is actually being very deliberate.

Now, last year was the exception that proves the rule. Green Bay made its big move when it traded for Micah Parsons. And it gave up two first-round picks to do it.

And look at that opportunity. A player of Parsons’ caliber becoming available is a once-in-a-decade event. Maybe even rarer. A four-time All-Pro. The first player in NFL history to start his career with five straight seasons of at least twelve sacks. You don’t get chances like that. So the Packers jumped. And I still think that was absolutely the right call.

But a move like that also changes how you operate afterward. Once you’ve spent that kind of capital, you don’t want to keep throwing even more picks into the fire. The Packers didn’t have a first-round pick in the 2026 draft for the first time in forty years. And they don’t have their 2027 first either. Both went to Dallas.

The Parsons trade was the splash. It was the big swing. And it’s a big part of why there was no second swing this week.

Think about the timing. A team that’s already made one franchise-altering trade isn’t going to rush into another one unless the case is overwhelming. The Parsons deal moved the Packers’ draft board in a huge way. And this front office knows that every additional trade compounds that effect. That’s discipline. And discipline rarely looks exciting on deadline day.

And now here’s the part I find absolutely fascinating.

Parsons still hasn’t played. He’s back at practice, his twenty-one-day practice window runs through November twenty-fifth, and the Rams game on Thanksgiving Eve could be a target for his return. But he hasn’t taken a snap yet this season.

So think about it. The Packers are six and three, winning five of six, scoring forty-five on the AFC champions, and they’re doing all of it without the player they paid two first-round picks to get.

Now look at all the teams that made trades this week. Over the next few days, a lot of them are going to have someone new walk into their building who can make a big impact. A new guard. A new defensive tackle. A new receiver.

The Packers are about to have the exact same thing happen. Except the new player is already on the roster. And he’s better than almost anybody who was available on the market.

When Micah Parsons comes back, that’s a major improvement. Bigger than nearly any trade the Packers could have realistically made. And it costs them nothing more in draft capital. For a front office that values its picks the way this one does, that’s about as good as it gets.

Imagine what it’s going to feel like when Parsons finally lines up. The Packers will have played most of the season without him and still be in the hunt. Then a player they gave up two first-rounders for steps onto the field next to Lukas Van Ness, in front of Evan Williams and Xavier McKinney, in Jonathan Gannon’s pressure scheme. That’s the kind of in-season boost no trade partner could have offered.

That’s why I don’t think the Packers didn’t care about this season. That’s just not the case. They have a way of operating. They like to hold onto their draft capital for the most part. And a lot of the time, it works out for them.

Now, compare that to other teams that made big moves. Some of those moves were about a major weakness. A team with a huge question mark at quarterback, for example, can watch an entire season slip away. That’s a need so big that you almost have to pay, whatever the cost. The hole forces the decision.

The Packers don’t have a hole like that. They have their quarterback. Jordan Love is playing at an MVP level. They have the receiving group, with Christian Watson and Matthew Golden, who just became the first duo in franchise history to each go for seven catches, one hundred fifty yards, and a touchdown in the same game. They have a great safety duo. And they have Parsons about to return.

When you look at all that, you recognize you’re a really good team. The big positions are covered. The remaining questions are matters of degree, not survival. And that belief is a big part of why the phone stayed quiet.

Now, let’s talk about the offensive line. Because that’s where a lot of people are going to push back. And honestly, it’s a fair point.

Would it have helped to add someone there? Sure. It definitely could have helped. A guy like Landon Dickerson, a three-time Pro Bowler, was available for what looked like a cheap price. But you still have to give away draft capital. And I don’t think the Packers were just one offensive lineman away from fixing all of their problems.

We’ve watched this offensive line get a lot better over the course of the year. With Laken Tomlinson at left guard and Kevin Zeitler finally in at right guard, it looks like they might have found something. The run blocking still hasn’t been great. I’m not going to pretend otherwise. But a single addition wasn’t going to turn the whole thing around overnight. And for the Packers, that’s a big reason not to pay for one.

It helps to separate deals into two categories. One kind of deal fills an emergency. The other kind makes a good team a little better. The Packers were shopping in the second category. And that’s exactly the category where discipline matters most. When you’re already winning, the value of one new piece is smaller. And the price you should be willing to pay shrinks right along with it.

Now, the big question. Jonathan Taylor.

It felt like a lot of teams were in the mix. And the Packers were connected to him for weeks. But in the end, the Colts made the decision to hold onto Taylor. According to Tom Pelissero, multiple teams offered a second-round pick and more. And the Colts turned them all down because they wanted a blockbuster.

Would it have made sense for Green Bay to go after him? I think so. It wouldn’t have been a one-season rental. He’s under contract for the next couple of seasons, so it would’ve been a long-term play. And yes, the offensive line hasn’t been great, but Jonathan Taylor is Jonathan Taylor. He’s incredibly productive. He would’ve helped this team, and he would’ve made the run game better. No doubt about it.

But there’s a lesson in how that played out. Taylor was the one name everybody connected to Green Bay. And when the Colts held on, there wasn’t an obvious second option that justified that kind of investment.

And a team that isn’t desperate doesn’t lower its standards just because its first choice wasn’t available. It waits. And in this case, the Packers waited all the way through the deadline.

The Colts traded away a lot of players. But they wanted to hold onto an upper-echelon running back. And honestly, I understand that. If you’re Indianapolis, why would you move the one guy who defines your offense? So the Packers didn’t get him. That’s not a failure. It’s just a market where the seller wasn’t selling.

And that fits the Packers’ pattern perfectly. It feels like almost every year, they’re in the mix on a few guys. Reports come out that Green Bay is looking into someone here or there. And I assume they really are, just to find out what the cost would be. That’s smart business. You make the call. You learn the price. You decide whether it’s worth it. And when the price is too high, you walk away.

That’s what happened this time.

Now, I do want to be fair to the other side of this. Because there’s one development that makes standing pat a little scarier. Josh Jacobs fractured his finger against New England. LaFleur said he had no grip strength, and now he needs surgery. Injured reserve hasn’t been ruled out, which would mean at least four games. So the run game, which finally showed signs of life with one hundred twenty-nine yards against the Patriots, is going to be tested without its lead back. MarShawn Lloyd, Kaleb Johnson, Pierre Strong, and Chris Brooks will have to step up.

That’s the risk of the quiet deadline. If the run game falls apart without Jacobs, people are going to point back at this week. That’s fair. But even then, I don’t think one rental running back was going to be the difference between a Super Bowl and an early exit.

Some teams are willing to go all in. If they feel the window is right now, they’ll sacrifice the future and spend a huge chunk of their draft capital to make the move. And the Packers aren’t afraid to do that. We saw it with Parsons. But they’re not a team that wants to bank everything on one season. And I think that’s one big reason they’ve had such consistent success. Because when you trade away a lot of your picks, especially the high ones, it hurts you down the road.

Think about the structure the Packers have chosen. Two enormous contracts at the top. One for the quarterback. One for the pass rusher. And then a roster around them that’s supposed to be filled with productive players on small salaries. That structure only works if the draft keeps producing. It’s a bet on scouting and development. And it’s a bet the Packers have been willing to make for a long time.

And this is where the money comes in. Because the money is the real reason this approach matters so much.

Jordan Love is making fifty-five million dollars a year. Micah Parsons is making more than forty million dollars a year. When you’re paying two players that kind of money, you have to fill the rest of the roster with players on cheap contracts. There’s no other way to make the math work.

That means you need draft capital. And you need to hit on your picks.

This past year, the Packers barely had any draft capital because of the Parsons deal. But next year, they’re going to have a lot more. Especially with all of those compensatory picks coming for players they lost in free agency, like Romeo Doubs, Malik Willis, and Quay Walker. Plus the extra fourth-round pick they got when they traded Rashan Gary to Dallas.

That’s a big deal. It means the front office is already thinking about refilling the supply. And a deadline trade this week would have worked against that.

That’s why the comp picks matter so much. They arrive without any trade. They’re the reward for players the team lost. And they refill the supply that the Parsons deal drained. When you see a team thinking that far ahead, a quiet deadline starts to look a lot more like part of a plan than a lack of one.

You can’t keep handing out huge deals and making big trades every single season. If the Packers did that every year, they wouldn’t have the cheap contracts surrounding their really expensive players. And when you hit on guys like Evan Williams in the fourth round, and Dani Dennis-Sutton in the later rounds, on those cheap deals, that’s what makes great teams even greater.

That’s the real edge. Not the flashy trade. The quiet hit in round four.

I understand that fans want action. In the short term, it would be fun to see big names come in every year. But sustained success requires patience. And if you look at the history of this franchise, that patience has paid off over and over again.

So here’s where I land. Brian Gutekunst’s decision to stand pat wasn’t a lack of ambition. It was confidence. Confidence in a roster that’s six and three. Confidence in a quarterback playing at an MVP level. Confidence in a defense coached by Jonathan Gannon. Confidence that Micah Parsons is the biggest trade deadline addition in the league, without costing a single extra pick. And confidence that a full stockpile of draft picks next spring will keep this machine running for years.

Now we find out if he was right. The Vikings are next on Sunday. A division rival. A must-win game in the best division in football. Without Josh Jacobs. And with Micah Parsons getting closer every day.
