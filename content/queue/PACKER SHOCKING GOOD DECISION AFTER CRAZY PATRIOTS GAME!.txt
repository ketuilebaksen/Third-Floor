Packers Nation, sometimes the biggest decision a head coach makes isn’t a trade. It isn’t a lineup change. It isn’t a fourth-down call. Sometimes the biggest decision is simply this. To stop fighting who you are. And to finally become who you’re supposed to be.

And against the New England Patriots, that’s exactly what Matt LaFleur and the Green Bay Packers did. The result? Forty-five to twenty-seven. The most points any opponent has ever scored at Gillette Stadium. And a performance so dominant that national analysts are now warning the rest of the NFC to beware of the Packers.

Welcome back to Third Floor. Today we’re talking about the decision behind the blowout. The decision that surprised a lot of people. The decision that turned this offense loose. And the decision that might define the rest of this season. Let’s get into it.

To understand why this decision was so big, you have to understand what Green Bay walked into. Because on paper, everything about this matchup told LaFleur to play it safe.

The Packers came into this game dead last in the entire NFL in rushing. Dead last. Four hundred eighty-five rushing yards through eight games. And they were facing a Mike Vrabel defense. And everybody around the league knows what a Vrabel defense does. It takes away what you do best. Whatever your strength is, that defense finds it and erases it.

So what’s Green Bay’s strength? The deep ball. Jordan Love throwing it down the field to Christian Watson and Matthew Golden. And the Patriots’ whole defensive philosophy starts with building a wall up front to stop the run. So one analyst explained it perfectly after the game. He said you knew Green Bay probably wasn’t going to run the ball on New England anyway. Which meant you expected the Patriots to focus everything on taking away the vertical passing game. Two-high safety looks. Keep everything in front. Make Jordan Love dink and dunk his way down the field.

In other words, New England’s defense was built to stop exactly what Green Bay wanted to do.

And on top of that, Jordan’s receivers were coming into this game leading the entire NFL in drops. Twenty-three as a team. Matthew Golden led the league individually with nine. So the safe decision, the conservative decision, would have been to back away. Shorten the throws. Take what the defense gives you. Avoid the risk of drops. Avoid the risk of turnovers against a defense designed to make you pay for going deep.

And Matt LaFleur and Jordan Love said no.

They went deep anyway.

And that’s the decision that shocked people. Even the analysts in the studio admitted they were surprised. Not by who won the game. By how it happened. One of them said Green Bay had been specializing in pushing the ball down the field the last couple of weeks, with two guys on the outside who can go get it vertically. And against a defense that was supposed to take that away, it just didn’t happen. The Patriots couldn’t stop it.

And then another analyst said the thing that I think explains this entire season.

He said the Packers were true to who they are. And that’s been a problem for Green Bay at times this year. Not being true to who they are. He said who they are is a deep-ball passing team. Take your shots down the field, and don’t back off. He said in some games, they backed away. On Sunday, they didn’t.

Packers Nation, think about the games this season where this team looked stuck. The loss in Detroit, where the offense stalled late after a huge defensive stop. The Carolina game, where a twenty-seven to ten lead turned into a twenty-seven to nineteen nail-biter because the offense went interception, three-and-out, three-and-out. In those moments, it felt like the offense got timid. Like it was trying to protect something instead of attacking.

Against New England, there was no timid. There was no backing away. There was just attack, attack, attack.

And look at the results.

It started on the very first snap. Xavier McKinney picked off Drake Maye on the Patriots’ first offensive play and returned it thirty-six yards. And on the Packers’ first offensive possession, Jordan Love scrambled for a thirteen-yard touchdown. Seven to nothing. Then Jordan hit Matthew Golden for a five-yard touchdown pass. The one hundredth touchdown pass of his career. Fourteen to nothing.

And from there, it was a fireworks show.

Jordan finished twenty-three of twenty-nine for three hundred seventy-two yards and three touchdown passes. Plus that rushing touchdown. Four total touchdowns. Matt LaFleur called it a career night in the locker room. And on throws that traveled at least twenty yards through the air, Jordan went six of nine for two hundred thirty-five yards and a touchdown. That’s the decision paying off right there. Six of nine on deep shots against a defense that was built to stop deep shots.

Matthew Golden, the receiver who came in leading the league in drops, had six catches for one hundred twenty-six yards and a touchdown in the first quarter alone. The most receiving yards by a Packer in an opening quarter since Antonio Freeman in 1998. Jordan hit him for forty-three yards. Then forty-nine yards. He didn’t stop throwing to Golden because of the drops. He kept trusting him. And Golden paid him back in a big way.

And Christian Watson, less than two weeks removed from a broken rib, caught a thirty-nine yard touchdown in the third quarter right after the Packers recovered a fumble. Strike right after the turnover. No hesitation.

And here’s the stat that LaFleur shared in the locker room that tells you just how historic this decision turned out to be. Matthew Golden and Christian Watson became the first duo in Packers history to each have at least seven receptions, one hundred fifty receiving yards, and a touchdown in the same game.

The first duo in franchise history. In a franchise that has had some of the greatest receivers in NFL history. That’s what happens when you stop backing away and let your playmakers go win.

One analyst said it looked like the Packers receivers were running seven-on-seven routes on air. Like no one was even covering them. He said the Patriots secondary just wasn’t there. He called out cornerback Christian Gonzalez, a player who just got paid, saying we haven’t seen him show up. And he said the list of New England’s problems in this game looked like a CVS receipt. It just kept going.

But here’s the part that people might miss. And it’s what makes this decision so smart. Being a deep-ball team doesn’t mean you never run the football.

The analyst who praised the Packers for staying true to themselves also said this. Build your lead by throwing the football. And if you need to run the ball to hold the lead, then run the ball.

And that’s exactly what happened. Because once the Packers built that lead through the air, the run game started to open up. LaFleur even joked with his players in the locker room. He started by saying, how about this, we rush for zero. And everybody laughed, because that’s what it’s felt like all year. But then he read the real number. Thirty-two attempts. One hundred twenty-nine yards. Two rushing touchdowns.

Think about that. The worst rushing team in the league ran for one hundred twenty-nine yards against a defense built to stop the run. How? Because the passing game made New England play scared. When safeties are worried about Watson and Golden getting behind them, they can’t creep up into the box. When the defense is down two touchdowns early, it can’t sit back and wait for you. The pass opened up the run. The deep ball created the space for the ground game.

That’s the beauty of the decision. LaFleur didn’t try to force the run to set up the pass. He let the pass set up the run. He leaned into his strength, and the strength fixed the weakness.

And even when Josh Jacobs left the game early with a hand injury, which is something we’ll have to keep an eye on, the run game kept working. Because the defense couldn’t commit to stopping it.

And this decision didn’t just change the offense. It changed the way the whole game unfolded for the defense too. When your offense puts up points fast, the other team has to throw. And when the other team has to throw against Jonathan Gannon’s pressure scheme, good things happen.

LaFleur told the team the defense came up with four takeaways. Two interceptions, from Xavier McKinney and Evan Williams. Isaiah McDuffie forced a fumble and recovered it himself. And the Packers were getting after Drake Maye all game long, with four sacks. All of that without Micah Parsons, who’s still working his way back at practice.

That’s complementary football. The offense attacks and scores. The other team falls behind and has to throw. The defense pins its ears back and takes the ball away. The offense gets the ball back on a short field and scores again. It’s a cycle. And on Sunday, the Packers rode that cycle to forty-five points.

And then there was the moment that showed just how much this performance changed the conversation around Jordan Love.

One of the analysts in the studio admitted on air that nobody had criticized Jordan Love more than he had. He said he used to ask, what are you about? Are you as good as your contract says? Are you as good as everybody makes you out to be?

And then he gave the answer. He is. He is delivering the goods.

He said even when the offensive line hasn’t played well, Jordan has stood in the pocket and made big-time throws off his back foot. He puts the football where only his receivers can get it. He keeps getting knocked down and he keeps getting back up. And he said Jordan Love is playing himself into the MVP conversation.

Another analyst agreed. He said we’re starting to see the real Jordan Love. This is him now. Let him go.

Let him go. That’s the decision in three words. Let Jordan Love go.

And one analyst made the MVP case even stronger. He said Jordan has done all of this with no threat of a run game for most of the season, meaning nobody respected the play-action. And with pass catchers who were leading the league in drops. And yet he kept pushing the ball to those receivers, showing them he believes in them. He said Jordan Love is lifting his team more than anybody. And to him, that’s what an MVP is.

Someone even asked if the Packers belong in the same category as the Bengals now. Throw it around, don’t bother running. And the analyst said he agreed more about the Packers, because of the level Jordan Love is playing at.

And then came the warning to the rest of the league. One analyst said it straight into the camera. Beware of the Packers. That’s his message to the rest of the NFC. They’re coming for you.

He admitted he usually picks the Packers to go to the Super Bowl every year. He didn’t this year. He picked the Cowboys instead. But he said, honestly, he’d rather have the Packers right now. And he pointed out the scariest part. The Packers still haven’t had Micah Parsons. And Parsons is coming back.

So now let’s talk about the next decision. Because it’s happening today.

It’s NFL trade deadline day. And the question is whether the Packers still need a running back after this performance.

The analysts debated it. One of them said it depends on the price. If the Packers can get Jonathan Taylor without selling their soul for him, they have to make that move. Because at some point in the fourth quarter of a big game, with a lead, you need to be able to run the football to close it out. And with Jonathan Taylor, they’d be more than capable.

But the price for Taylor has reportedly been sky-high. The Colts have been asking for a first-round pick and more, or something close to the old Christian McCaffrey package of a second, a third, a fourth, and a fifth. And the Packers already gave up two first-round picks for Micah Parsons.

The cheaper options are still out there. Tony Pollard in Tennessee, with a reported asking price around a third or fourth-round pick. Tyjae Spears, also in Tennessee, even cheaper. And on the line, Landon Dickerson from Philadelphia, a three-time Pro Bowler who’d cost around seven hundred forty-two thousand dollars against the cap for the rest of this season.

And here’s my take after the Patriots game. The decision LaFleur made on Sunday tells you what this team should do at the deadline too. Don’t try to become something you’re not. Don’t trade a fortune for a superstar running back to turn this into a ground-and-pound team. This is a passing team. A deep-ball team. Jordan Love’s team.

But add a piece that helps you close games. A back like Pollard, who can run the ball when you have the lead and catch passes out of the backfield. A guard like Dickerson, who makes the line more stable for both the run and the pass. Smart moves that support who you already are. Not moves that try to change it.

Because who this team is right now is pretty special.

Six and three. Five wins in their last six games. Forty-five points against the defending AFC champions. A quarterback in the MVP conversation. Two receivers who just made franchise history. A defense that just forced four turnovers. A rookie kicker who went seven for seven. A run game that finally showed up because the passing game opened the door. And a superstar pass rusher on the way back.

And next up is Minnesota. The Vikings beat the Packers in week one, scoring twenty-nine unanswered points after Green Bay led twenty-two to ten. That’s a revenge game. And if LaFleur keeps making the same decision he made in New England, staying aggressive, staying true to who this team is, I like the Packers’ chances.

So here’s the message, Packers Nation. The shocking decision wasn’t really shocking at all. It was the obvious decision. The one we’ve been waiting for all season. Trust Jordan Love. Trust Watson and Golden. Attack. Don’t back away.

And when you do that, you score forty-five points in Foxborough. You make history. And you send a warning to the entire NFC.
