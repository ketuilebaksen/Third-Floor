Packers Nation, the trade deadline is getting closer by the day, and one of the most elite running backs in the entire NFL keeps getting connected to Green Bay. Jonathan Taylor. A guy who can take over a game by himself. A guy who could turn this offense into something truly terrifying. But before we start dreaming about him in green and gold, we need to talk about why this rumor even exists. Because it all goes back to that frustrating twenty to seventeen loss in Detroit.

Welcome back to Third Floor. Today we’re doing a full breakdown. Three encouraging performances from the Lions game. Three real disappointments. And then the big question: should the Packers make a blockbuster trade for Jonathan Taylor, or is the real problem somewhere else? Let’s get into it.

Let me start with the short version of how the Packers lost in Detroit. Eight pressures allowed by one starting guard. Two missed field goals by a rookie kicker. And an offense that couldn’t capitalize on a fourth-down stop with the game tied. That’s how you lose twenty to seventeen. And it’s frustrating, because Green Bay had chances to win this game.

Here’s what a lot of people miss when they just look at the score. Games like this are rarely lost on one play. They’re lost through a series of small moments, each one looking manageable on its own, that add up to a result. A missed kick here. A blown block there. An offense that stalls at exactly the wrong time. The Packers are good enough to win games like this, and that’s why it hurts so much. They didn’t lose to a better team on the day. They lost to their own mistakes.

But let’s start with the positives, because there were some real ones.

Positive number one. The rookie cornerback.

He was facing a dangerous Detroit passing attack, with weapons all over the field. And according to early numbers from Pro Football Focus, he allowed six completions on eight targets for sixty-seven yards. Now, on paper, that might not jump out at you. But here’s the part that matters. He prevented two potential touchdowns against Jameson Williams, including a diving breakup in the end zone. And he even had an interception wiped out by a pass interference penalty.

For a young corner facing that kind of competition, that’s an encouraging performance. It shows he’s willing to compete on the biggest plays. And that’s what matters. Giving up some completions is normal when you’re facing talented receivers. What you watch is what happens in the big moments. Preventing touchdowns. Contesting the football. Staying aggressive. Those are the traits that earn a coaching staff’s trust. And remember, LaFleur said this week that he’s mentally tough, that he’s wired the right way, and that corners have to be resilient and bounce back. Rookies who show those habits early tend to grow fast, because the staff gives them more chances and the confidence follows.

Positive number two. Anthony Campbell.

With Javon Hargrave and Warren Brinson dealing with long-term injuries, the Packers needed somebody on the defensive line to step up. And Campbell did. He had the team’s only sack, two tackles for loss, and a pass breakup. That’s exactly the kind of production this defense needs from its depth.

Depth on the defensive line is one of the least glamorous parts of a roster, but it’s one of the most important. Injuries are constant at that position. The teams that handle them best are the ones whose backups come in and play with energy. Campbell’s game tells you the coaching staff has a player who can handle a bigger role. And a good rotation also keeps the starters fresh, which matters a lot over a long season.

Positive number three. The run defense.

Jahmyr Gibbs got to one hundred six rushing yards, but it took him twenty-nine carries to get there. And Detroit as a team managed just one hundred nineteen rushing yards on thirty-three attempts. That’s three point six yards per carry against one of the best rushing attacks in the league.

And late in the fourth quarter, with the game tied at seventeen, the Packers stopped the Lions on fourth down deep in Detroit territory. That should have been the moment Green Bay grabbed the game by the throat.

Think about what stopping the run does for a defense. When a team can’t run the ball, it has to throw. And when it has to throw, your pass rushers can pin their ears back and attack. That’s how you create the pressure and turnovers that swing games. Holding a back as talented as Gibbs to a modest average, even with all that volume, shows the front is doing its job. Which makes what happened next even harder to accept.

Now, the disappointments.

Disappointment number one. The kicker.

Trey Smack had a nightmare afternoon. He missed a twenty-six yard field goal early. Then late in the fourth quarter, he failed on a thirty-five yarder, with Detroit’s D.J. Reed credited with blocking it. Two failed field goals in a three-point loss. You can’t ignore that.

Matt LaFleur defended his kicker afterward, and he said this week that they’ve seen enough of a sample size and enough progress through the offseason to feel good about him. It’s a next-game mentality. But Brian Gutekunst has a tough decision to think about. Green Bay gave up two draft picks to move up and get this kicker. The team needs more consistency from him.

And kickers are some of the most psychologically fragile players in football. Their whole job is to succeed in isolated moments with everyone watching. A rookie who misses early can carry that doubt into his next attempt. The coaching staff has to find the balance between support and accountability. And whatever they decide will affect how this team approaches fourth downs and field goal range for the rest of the season. Remember, LaFleur admitted he regretted going for it on fourth and four near the goal line instead of kicking the tying field goal. When you don’t fully trust your kicker, it changes every decision.

Disappointment number two. Travis Glover.

The Packers kept Glover at right guard instead of starting veteran Kevin Zeitler. And according to PFF’s initial evaluation, Glover allowed eight quarterback pressures. Eight. That’s half of what Zeitler reportedly allowed during the entire previous season. The exact number varied a bit across reports, but the message was the same. The right guard spot was a big problem in Detroit.

And it’s worth thinking about how a coaching staff makes these calls. Starting a younger player with more upside can pay off over time. But a veteran with a proven track record in pass protection might be the safer choice in a tight game. When the results show the safer choice would have helped, the staff has to adjust. That’s not a failure. That’s just managing a roster. The best staffs react quickly when the evidence shows up. And now, with Glover dealing with a knee injury, Zeitler might finally get his shot anyway.

Disappointment number three. The run game.

Josh Jacobs returned after missing the first six games, and his overall day looked okay. Ninety-one yards from scrimmage, including a twenty-five yard receiving touchdown. But on the ground, he had just thirty-six yards on eleven carries. And as a team, the Packers ran for sixty yards on nineteen attempts. That’s simply not enough.

And I don’t think Jacobs deserves all the blame for that. The line has to create better lanes. Jordan Love finished with two hundred seventy-four passing yards, two touchdowns, and an interception. But after the defense came up with that huge fourth-down stop, the offense couldn’t score. Detroit got the ball back, drove down the field, and won it on a twenty-two yard field goal from Jake Bates. The Packers had their chance, and they didn’t finish the job.

There’s also the reality of a back returning after missing so much time. Jacobs sat out the first six games. It takes time to rebuild your rhythm, your vision, your trust in the guys blocking for you. A player in that spot might hesitate for a fraction of a second, and in the run game, that’s the difference between a four-yard gain and a loss. I expect his numbers to climb as he gets comfortable. But the line has to give him room.

And that brings us to the big one. Jonathan Taylor.

According to reporting attributed to ESPN’s Adam Schefter, Green Bay is among the teams connected to a possible trade for the Colts’ star running back, along with Seattle, Minnesota, and Denver. Now, I want to be crystal clear. No trade has been completed. There’s no confirmed formal offer from the Packers.

And it’s worth remembering how these rumors work. A report that a team is connected to a player can come from an agent, from a team executive, or from a reporter reading the market. Sometimes it just means somebody called somebody. The Packers haven’t confirmed interest. The Colts haven’t confirmed Taylor is available. And nothing about a price is public. So everything we talk about here is a possibility, not a plan.

But man, is it an exciting possibility.

What makes it even more interesting is the history. Back in 2023, the Packers explored a deal for Taylor, and Indianapolis reportedly wanted Christian Watson and more. The deal never happened. And look at Watson now. He’s signed a four-year, ninety-two million dollar contract with Green Bay and become one of the most important players in this offense. So imagine this. Getting Jonathan Taylor without giving up Christian Watson. That’s the dream scenario.

And what a player Taylor is. He ran for fourteen hundred thirty-one yards in 2024 and kept producing at a high level after that. This season, the numbers cited show four hundred ninety-two yards on one hundred twenty-two carries. He’s one of the most talented backs in the league, he can carry a big workload, and when he gets going, he can break a game open with one run.

Now picture him in this offense. Jordan Love at quarterback. Christian Watson and Matthew Golden on the outside. Tucker Kraft finding his rhythm again. Savion Williams being the goon LaFleur wants him to be. And Jonathan Taylor in the backfield. Defenses would have no idea what to do. Load the box to stop Taylor, and Watson burns you deep. Play two safeties deep, and Taylor gashes you for six yards a carry. That’s the kind of balance that wins in January.

But there’s the cost. Taylor signed a two-year, forty-four million dollar extension. That’s a big financial commitment. And Indianapolis would likely want significant compensation. Since a deal involving Watson is obviously off the table now, any offer would probably be built around draft picks. And remember, the Packers don’t have their 2027 first-round pick because of the Micah Parsons trade. So every pick they do have matters.

There’s also speculation that the Colts’ front office changes could make them willing to move veterans before the November tenth deadline. But there’s no guarantee Taylor actually becomes available.

And that raises the Josh Jacobs question. Jacobs signed a four-year, forty-eight million dollar deal in 2024. He ran for thirteen hundred twenty-nine yards that season, then dropped to nine hundred twenty-nine in 2025. The report suggests Green Bay could save around thirteen million in cap space next year by moving on from him. But there’s no confirmation that the Packers intend to do that.

And there’s a human side to this too. A veteran running back who gets replaced, or asked to share carries, is going to feel his role changing. Jacobs is a competitor. LaFleur just said he brings physicality, toughness, and an edge to this whole team. If a new back arrives, the staff would need to manage that relationship carefully. That matters as much as the cap numbers.

So here’s my honest take, Packers Nation.

Taylor is outstanding. Adding him next to Love and Watson would make this offense even more dangerous. No doubt about it. But would he solve the biggest problem? After watching the Detroit game, I keep coming back to the offensive line. Jacobs is still talented. And if the blocking doesn’t improve, even Jonathan Taylor could struggle to find room.

Think about how the run game actually works. The running back’s job is to read the blocks and hit the right lane. But the lane has to exist first. If the line creates it consistently, even an average back will produce, and an elite one will break big plays. If the line doesn’t create it, a great back can only make yards on his own, and that’s not sustainable over a whole game. That’s why running back trades often look bigger than they really are, unless the line is already doing its job.

And there’s the timing issue. A back acquired at the deadline only has a few weeks to learn the offense, build chemistry with the line, and find his rhythm. That’s a shorter runway than most people think. It argues against expecting instant results.

So my approach would be this. Fix the line first. Get Kevin Zeitler in there. Find the best five. Look at upgrades up front that help every running back and the quarterback too. And if, on top of that, the Colts make Jonathan Taylor available at a price that doesn’t wreck the future? Then go get him. Because a fixed line plus Jonathan Taylor? That’s not just a good offense. That’s the scariest offense in the NFL.

The deadline isn’t just about adding a big name. It’s about making the right move at the right time. Green Bay has a talented quarterback, dangerous receivers, a run defense that’s holding up, and a defense that’s about to potentially get Micah Parsons back. This team is close. But against teams like Detroit, small mistakes decide everything. A missed block. A failed field goal. One wasted possession. Fix those, and this team can beat anyone.
